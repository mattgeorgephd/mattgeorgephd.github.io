## Approach

Access to nature is not evenly shared, and losing everyday contact with nature, sometimes called the "extinction of experience," weakens people's connection to the environment and their concern for it.<sup>[1](#ref-1)</sup> Students' interest in environmental science grows from experiences that build confidence that they can do the work,<sup>[2](#ref-2)</sup> so I see engagement as part of the research itself rather than an add-on. I work toward a community of practice: researchers, growers, tribes, agencies, schools, and the public collaborating on shared questions about the health of local ecosystems, rather than one-way outreach.

That has meant building partnerships across sectors, with Taylor Shellfish, the Jamestown S'Klallam Tribe, Bellingham Technical College, Puget Sound Restoration Fund, and NOAA, to study how physical drivers affect ecosystem health in the Salish Sea. In practice it looks like:

- **Bringing young people to the shore.** As a Seattle Aquarium Beach Naturalist (2014-2018), I talked with K-12 students and visitors about how watersheds shape marine ecosystems and mentored K-12 students through the aquarium's Youth Ocean Advocates program.
- **Opening field science to the public.** After the June 2021 heat dome coincided with extreme low tides, I volunteered on Multi-Agency Rocky Intertidal Network (MARINe) surveys documenting the damage to intertidal invertebrate populations. Citizen science is one of the most promising ways to restore people's contact with nature,<sup>[1](#ref-1)</sup> and the scale of that event showed how much trained community volunteers could add to monitoring as marine heatwaves become more frequent.
- **Working with tribal partners.** Collaborating with the Jamestown S'Klallam Tribe on climate impacts to shellfish taught me how much Indigenous knowledge and priorities strengthen ecological research, and I aim to center questions that matter to tribal co-managers.
- **Mentoring for access.** Through programs including NSF's Louis Stokes Alliance for Minority Participation, I have mentored students from backgrounds underrepresented in science, and in teaching I highlight the contributions of women and scientists of color.
